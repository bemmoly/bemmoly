Bemmoly Pipelines

The place an engineering organisation ships software from. Every service in the company, who owns it, what is running where, and how a change gets from a commit to production in every region, with the rules each team agreed to. It runs on your own servers and deploys to the cloud you choose.

Proposal, draft 2 16 October 2026 Separate product, own shell Designed for 500 engineers and 1,000 services Architecture doc
What changed from draft 1. Pipelines is now its own product with its own shell, not a section of Bemmoly's sidebar, so people who only use Work and Docs never see it. The design starts from a 500-engineer company with 50 teams and about 1,000 repositories, and still fits a five-person startup. Google Cloud is the first cloud, behind a provider model that AWS and others plug into.

The product

A separate product, with the Bemmoly family's foundations (sign-in, people, teams, single sign-on, audit, type, colour, icons) and its own shell, at its own address (pipelines.acme.dev). A company can run Pipelines on its own, or next to Bemmoly Work and Docs on the same install. Either way it is a different front door. Someone with Work access and no Pipelines access never sees a Pipelines link, a menu item or a notification.

Organising 1,000 repositories

With 1,000 repositories, a flat list stops working, and a single folder tree breaks the first time a reorganisation happens. Pipelines uses three separate axes. Each one answers a single question and changes at its own pace.

Where it sits: the catalog

Domain › System › Service. Commerce › Checkout › checkout-api. This changes rarely, follows the architecture, and is how people browse.

Who owns it: teams

Every service has exactly one owning team, taken from the identity provider. Teams change often, and ownership moves without moving the service.

How careful to be: tiers

Tier 0 (money, sign-in), Tier 1 (customer-facing), Tier 2 (internal). Policies attach to tiers, so 41 Tier 0 services share one rule.

On top of these there are free labels for anything else: language, runtime, lifecycle (experimental, production, deprecated), compliance scope. Saved views turn any combination into a list people return to, such as "Tier 0 in Commerce" or "Not deployed in 90 days".

# services/checkout-api/.bemmoly/service.yaml: the catalog lives with the code name: checkout-api system: checkout # system and domain come from the catalog owner: team/payments # a team from the identity provider tier: 0 labels: [node, pci] pipeline: { extends: acme/node-service@3 } # the platform team's golden path

How the catalog fills itself. Installing the GitHub App on the organisation scans every repository. It finds deployable things (Dockerfiles, Helm charts, serverless manifests, monorepo workspaces), reads CODEOWNERS and any existing catalog files, and proposes services with owners. A platform engineer accepts them in bulk. Pipelines opens one pull request per repository with service.yaml. Services without an owner appear in a No owner view and cannot deploy to Prod until someone claims them.

Repositories are not services. A monorepo holds 40 services. Each run builds only what the commit touched (affected-service detection from the workspace graph), so one push to a monorepo does not start 40 pipelines.

Who can do what

At 500 people, nobody grants access to individuals. Access comes from three things together, and the product can always explain the result.

  1. Roles bound to teams at a scope. Roles: Viewer, Developer, Maintainer, Release manager, Platform admin, Auditor. A scope is the organisation, a domain, a system or a service. A binding at Commerce applies to everything under it, unless a narrower binding says otherwise.
  2. Ownership. The owning team is Maintainer of its services by default. Everyone else in engineering is Viewer. Ownership alone covers most of the access people need.
  3. Policies on environments. These are rules that hold whatever someone's role is. For example: "Tier 0 to Prod needs two approvals from the owning team and one from SRE on call, a passing canary, and the Mon to Thu 10:00 to 16:00 window; never during a freeze." They are written in plain sentences and stored as code.
RoleCanTypical binding
ViewerSee services, runs, logs (secrets masked), releasesEveryone in engineering, at the organisation
DeveloperRun pipelines, re-run, deploy to non-protected environments, request ProdOwning team (implicit), contributing teams per system

"Why can't I?" always has an answer. Every refused action names the rule and who can grant it, for example: "Tier 0 to Prod needs a Release manager in Commerce. Ask Payments release managers." The Access screen also has a checker. Pick a person, an action and a target, and it shows the decision with every binding and policy that produced it.

Keep your CI, or don't

Most companies with 500 engineers already have a CI system, such as GitHub Actions, Jenkins or Buildkite, and years of build scripts in it. Pipelines does not ask them to give that up. A build is only the first step of a release. What these companies lack is everything after the build, so that is where Pipelines starts. There are three ways in, and they can be mixed service by service:

ModeWho builds and testsWhat Pipelines doesHow it connects
Bring your CI

So the answer to "do they have to leave GitHub CI?" is no. Bring your CI is a full, first-class mode, not a stepping stone. Teams move builds onto Pipelines' runners only when they want something only the runners give them: one queue with fair share across the company, faster cached builds on their own cloud, or one place to read a failed build and its deploy together. The Run screen shows a run from GitHub Actions too, with its jobs, their status and a link to the full log there, so the journey from commit to Prod is complete either way.

Getting to production

A release is one built image plus its configuration. It is created once and promoted unchanged along the environment path: Dev, Staging, then Prod in each region. Teams choose how a promotion happens, per service, within what their tier's policy allows.

ModeHowFits
ContinuousPromotes on its own when every gate passesTier 2, most Tier 1 Dev and Staging
On approvalGates pass, then named approvers decideTier 0 and Tier 1 Prod
Release trainJoins the next scheduled train (for example 11:00 and 16:00); the train goes out as one coordinated rollout with one approvalDomains that release together; mobile backends
Coordinated releaseSeveral services in a stated order (schema migration, then API, then web), stopping the set if one failsCross-service changes

Gates are checks the policy requires. Each one is shown as pass, fail or waiting, with its reason: tests, security scan, a migration reviewed, error budget remaining, no freeze, the deploy window, approvals, a canary verdict, and dependencies already out. Rollout waves cover regions. The first region gets a canary (5%, then 25%, then 100%, each step judged on metrics). Later regions follow in parallel after a wait. Roll back is one action from anywhere. It restores the previous release's revision in seconds, and the rollback is recorded as a deployment like any other.

Thousands of runs at once

At peak, Acme has 300 jobs running and a queue behind them. The product shows the queue as plainly as the jobs. The rules that keep waits short are visible too:

  • Fair share by team. Each team has a guaranteed share of runners and can borrow idle capacity. One team's monorepo rebuild can't starve everyone else.
  • Priority. Prod deploys and roll backs go first, then main, then pull requests. A roll back never waits behind a pull request build.
  • Superseding. A new push to a pull request cancels that pull request's older run. A newer release waiting for Prod replaces an older one that is still waiting.
  • One deploy at a time per service and environment, queued in order, so two deploys never race each other.
  • Only what changed. Affected-service detection in monorepos, remote build caches, and test splitting across shards by past timing.
  • Every wait has a reason on screen: "Waiting for a linux-x64 runner, 3rd in Payments' queue, about 40 s", or "Waiting for the 11:00 train".

An environment can belong to any provider, so Acme's acquired company can have prod-aws-us-east-1 beside prod-gcp-asia-south1 on the same release path.

Signature moments

The parts people will show each other. Each one solves a real problem first.

The Release map

The whole company's delivery on one screen. Domains are rows and environments are columns. Releases move across as dots in real time: blue while running, amber while waiting, red when failing. Drag the timeline back to see exactly what was running at 14:05 yesterday.

Journeys, not job lists

Your change is a line from commit to every Prod region, with the current station live. "Where is my change?" takes one glance, not five tabs.

What changed?

When something breaks, every deploy, config change, infrastructure change and access grant from the last hours sits on one timeline, laid over the error graph. Each item can be rolled back from that timeline.

Promotion you can trust

One screen holds the diff (commits, issues, migrations, config), every gate with its reason, the approvers, and the rollout plan across regions. You approve once, and you know exactly what you approved.

Explain anything

"Why is this waiting?", "Why can't I deploy?", "Why did this fail?" always have a plain answer. With AI on, a failed step comes with the line that failed and a suggested fix.

Golden paths at fleet scale

The platform team updates node-service to v4 and sees 612 services on v3. One action opens pull requests to all of them, in waves, and the fleet view shows who has upgraded.

The shell

Pipelines' own frame. It is built for scanning lots of live information, and it is clearly a member of the Bemmoly family.

  • A 72px rail on the left with seven places: For you, Map, Catalog, Runs, Releases, Insights, Platform. Each has an icon and an 11px label, because people new to the product read before they memorise. Settings and the person menu sit at its foot.
  • A 48px top bar with the scope on the left (Acme › Commerce › Checkout, a searchable switcher at every level), search in the middle (services, runs, commits, people, with Mod+K for commands), the live counter ("214 running · 38 queued"), notifications and help.
  • Scope is the main idea. Map, Catalog, Runs, Releases and Insights all show "this scope". Zooming from the organisation to a domain to a service keeps you on the same screen at a finer grain, like zooming a map.
  • Dark by default, because engineers keep it open beside an editor and a terminal. Light is one keypress away, and every screen is designed in both.
  • Command first. Mod+K can do anything: "deploy checkout-api to staging", "roll back pricing-svc in us-east1", "who owns search-api". Every action on screen has a command and a key.

The product mark is the Bemmoly mark with the word "Pipelines". When both products are installed, people with access to both see a product switcher in the top bar. Nobody else does.

Design foundations

Pipelines keeps everything in docs/design/system that is not the frame: tokens, type scale, radii, elevation, motion, icons, the glyph ban, content rules, accessibility, and the rule that AI lilac appears only when AI is on. It replaces only the frame. That needs an ADR, "Pipelines is a separate product with its own shell", and a short section in the design system: docs/design/pipelines/ covering the shell, scope and the new components.

Questions for you

  1. One install or two? I recommend the same codebase and kernel (sign-in, teams, SSO, SCIM, audit), with Pipelines as its own web app and host. A company can run Pipelines alone or beside Work and Docs. A fully separate repository would duplicate identity and audit.
  2. Catalog words. Domain › System › Service, plus teams and tiers. Do these fit how you and Kero talk, or would "Group" and "Application" read better?
  3. Where do policies live? I recommend Pipelines as the source of truth, with export to a repository for review. The alternative is policies only as code in a platform repository.
  4. Lead with Bring your CI for larger companies? I recommend it: they adopt Pipelines for releases without touching their builds, and move builds later if they want. The risk is that a company never moves builds, and that is fine.
  5. Runners by default. I recommend autoscaled runners in the customer's Kubernetes or VMs, which work the same on every cloud, with serverless build services as an option. Is "no cluster on day one" important enough to keep Cloud Build as the startup default?
  6. The three promises still hold. Open source, no subscription, no in-app purchases. The only bill is the customer's cloud. Is that still the deal for Pipelines?

Acme is a fictional company; its people, services and numbers are examples.
